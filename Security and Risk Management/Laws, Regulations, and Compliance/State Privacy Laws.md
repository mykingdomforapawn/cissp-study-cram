In the absence of a single comprehensive US federal privacy law (see [[Laws]] for the sector-specific federal laws that exist instead), individual states have started passing their own general-purpose privacy statutes, creating a patchwork that organizations operating across states must reconcile.

- **CCPA/CPRA (California Consumer Privacy Act / California Privacy Rights Act)** — the flagship example and first mover; grants California residents rights to know what data is collected, request deletion, and opt out of sale of their data. CPRA expanded CCPA and created a dedicated enforcement agency.
- Other states (e.g., Virginia, Colorado) have followed with broadly similar but not identical frameworks.

The practical challenge is that these laws differ in scope, thresholds, and specific rights granted, so a business operating nationally often ends up complying with the strictest applicable state law across the board rather than tracking each one separately.

## Data Breach Notification Laws

A separate, older category of state law from the general-purpose privacy statutes above (California passed the first one, which GDPR was itself later modeled in part after). These laws require notifying affected individuals when their personal information is exposed in a breach, and generally apply when a person's name appears alongside a defined set of sensitive identifiers — typically Social Security number, driver's license or state ID number, and credit/debit card or bank account number. Other identifiers, such as a student ID number, are generally not covered.
